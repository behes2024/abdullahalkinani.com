Research

Research program

Freshwater invasions rarely happen in isolation. In Oregon rivers, non-native Smallmouth Bass (Micropterus dolomieu) interact with both native Signal Crayfish (Pacifastacus leniusculus) and the expanding non-native Ringed Crayfish (Faxonius neglectus). My dissertation examines these interactions across three linked studies — from diet patterns and prey availability in the field, to predator–prey mechanisms in the laboratory, to how temperature shapes competition between the two crayfish species.

The question running through all three chapters: can interactions among non-native species alter the course of a biological invasion, and what does that mean for the native species caught between them?

Study species

Smallmouth Bass
Micropterus dolomieu

Non-native predator established in the Willamette and Umpqua Rivers, and the focal fish species in the diet and predator–prey research (Chapters 1–2).

Signal Crayfish

Native crayfish of the Pacific Northwest, and the native prey and competitor species examined throughout the dissertation.

Ringed Crayfish

Non-native crayfish expanding into Oregon’s Umpqua and Rogue basins, and the focal invader in the predator–prey and competition experiments (Chapters 2–3).

Dissertation chapters

Chapter 1

Trophic ecology of Smallmouth Bass in two Oregon rivers

Research questions.

  • How important are crayfish, relative to fish and invertebrates, in Smallmouth Bass diets in the Willamette and Umpqua Rivers, and does that pattern change across years?
  • In the Umpqua River, where Signal and Ringed Crayfish co-occur, does Smallmouth Bass show species-specific electivity when diet is compared with local crayfish availability?
  • How variable is diet composition among years within each river?

Approach. Diet samples come from the Willamette River Fish Decadal Assessment and the annual Umpqua Bass Bash, paired with a standardized capture–mark–recapture crayfish availability study across five Umpqua sites, sampled over four days each. Comparing stomach contents with local prey availability allows me to calculate Jacobs’ electivity index (D) and test whether Smallmouth Bass select for Ringed over Signal Crayfish rather than simply consuming whichever is more available.

Study area. The Willamette and Umpqua River basins, Oregon — two systems that differ in geomorphology, thermal regime, and crayfish assemblage, providing a natural contrast for these comparisons.

Chapter 2

Laboratory predator–prey trials: Smallmouth Bass and crayfish

When native Signal Crayfish and non-native Ringed Crayfish are offered at equal size and density, do Smallmouth Bass show a species-specific feeding preference — and how do temperature and refuge availability change that outcome?

Experimental design. Trials run in parallel across a six-tank system, each tank a glass aquarium (120 × 40 × 50 cm) divided into three compartments that isolate the predator, a shared arena, and the prey until the trial begins. Each trial offers one Smallmouth Bass a simultaneous choice between a size-matched Signal and Ringed Crayfish. Bass are size-standardized (150–250 mm TL) and individually tracked across a maximum of two trials, with treatment order randomized and a 48-hour washout between trials.

Measurements. Predation occurrence, species consumed, attack latency, handling time, and refuge occupancy — recorded on video and used to test whether prey choice tracks optimal foraging (shorter handling time) rather than prey origin alone, and whether warmer water or reduced refuge changes predation rates or the direction of species-specific vulnerability.

Preliminary observations. Early trials show substantial individual variation in Smallmouth Bass feeding behavior, underscoring the value of repeated trials and standardized conditions before drawing conclusions about species-specific preference.

Chapter 3

Thermal effects on native–non-native crayfish competition

Research questions.

  • How does interspecific shelter competition between Signal and Ringed Crayfish vary with relative body size and sex?
  • How does thermal context change activity levels, encounter rates, and the strength of competitive asymmetry between the two species?
  • Does displacement from shelter measurably increase a crayfish’s behavioral exposure?

Field component. Umpqua Basin sites are classified into cold (≤18 °C), transitional (18–21 °C), and warm (≥21 °C) reaches using a modeled stream temperature dataset, validated with in-situ loggers, then sampled with standardized baited traps to characterize crayfish coexistence and catch-per-unit-effort by species, size, sex, and molt stage.

Competition experiments. A fully factorial design crosses two temperatures (cool 17 °C, warm 23 °C) with two shelter levels — one refuge, forcing direct competition, versus two refuges — for size-matched Signal and Ringed Crayfish pairs, recording shelter dominance, aggression, and displacement over 60-minute video-recorded trials.

Thermal preference trials. Individual crayfish are placed in a temperature-controlled apparatus and allowed to move freely between cool and warm zones, with position tracked on video to quantify preferred temperature, thermal niche breadth, and activity level for each species.

Video from both experiments is scored in BORIS, with movement paths extracted using ToxTrac, Fiji/TrackMate, and DeepLabCut.
